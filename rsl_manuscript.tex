\begin{abstract}
Few-label comparisons of deep models and gradient boosting for hyperspectral classification depend on how the data are split and prepared. We compare LightGBM with three transformers and the 3-D CNN HybridSN under leaky and controlled protocols, using five paired seeds and false-discovery-rate control. On Indian Pines, a random pixel split puts HybridSN 17--20 percentage points (pp) ahead of LightGBM from 5\% labelled pixels; on spatially disjoint tiles no deep model is significantly better than LightGBM. On Pavia University and Salinas, a random split raises the accuracy of LightGBM and HybridSN by 3--11\pp\ but leaves the ranking unchanged: HybridSN stays ahead under disjoint tiles (+3 to +9\pp\ over LightGBM; seven of eight budgets without extra validation labels). On laboratory images of Indian soils, 38--72\% of patches show only the sample tray, and a classifier trained on tray patches alone reaches 87\% accuracy (chance 33\%). Removing the tray and normalising brightness lifts every model to a mean of about 94\% or more with one labelled sample per class and removes most differences between them. Spatially disjoint splits, background masking and a tray-only check should precede any ranking.
\end{abstract}

Hyperspectral imaging; data leakage; spatially disjoint split; LightGBM; transformer; soil classification

\section{Introduction}
Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) records hundreds of narrow spectral bands and is used for agricultural and soil surveys \citep{Bioucas2013}. Labels are expensive, so classifiers are usually trained on a small share of the pixels, and the choice between deep models \citep{Roy2020,Hong2022} and simpler learners such as gradient-boosted trees \citep{Ke2017,Grinsztajn2022} matters in practice.

Such comparisons can be distorted in two ways. \emph{Spatial leakage}: with a random pixel split, training and test patches overlap, which favours patch-based models \citep{Liang2017,Nalepa2019}. \emph{Acquisition leakage}: in laboratory imaging, background pixels and session-specific illumination can carry the class label, so a model may recognise the imaging session rather than the material, a form of shortcut learning \citep{Lapuschkin2019,Geirhos2020}. The first effect is known but still common; the second is rarely checked.

We measure how much each effect changes the ranking of seven learners at small annotation budgets. Our contributions are: (i) the same models, seeds and statistics under a random and a spatially disjoint split of three airborne scenes; (ii) for a laboratory soil dataset, a comparison of raw, soil-only and brightness-normalised patches, a tray-only leakage check and a moist/dry cross-state test; and (iii) a short checklist for few-label HSI comparisons.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\emph{Airborne scenes.} Indian Pines (AVIRIS; $145\times145$ pixels, 200 bands after water-band removal, 16 classes, 10\,249 labelled pixels; \citealt{Baumgardner2015}), Pavia University (ROSIS; $610\times340$ pixels, 103 bands, 9 classes, 42\,776 labelled pixels; \citealt{Gamba2004}) and Salinas (AVIRIS; $512\times217$ pixels, 204 bands, 16 classes, 54\,129 labelled pixels; \citealt{GIC2021}). Indian Pines band centres are taken from the 1992 AVIRIS calibration \citep{Baumgardner2015}.

\emph{Local Indian soil.} Soil samples from Maharashtra, Gujarat and Hisar (Haryana), provided by the ICAR-National Bureau of Soil Survey and Land Use Planning (NBSS\&LUP), were imaged in the laboratory with a Resonon Pika NIR-320 line-scan camera (320 spatial pixels, 168 bands, 888.7--1714.6\,nm). We use the Black, Red and Yellow soil folders (36 images). Each image shows six portions of one sample on a bright tray. Values are raw digital numbers: no white or dark reference frames are available. Soil type is confounded with acquisition session and moisture: all Black-soil images were acquired moist, all Yellow-soil images dry on one date, and Red soil both dry (one date) and moist. Moist and dry Red images share sample numbers, so images with the same number are treated as one sample (22 samples: 7 Black, 8 Red, 7 Yellow) in the soil-only protocols.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}


\subsection{Models and training}
\textbf{LightGBM} \citep{Ke2017}, a \textbf{random forest} (RF, 500 trees) and an RBF \textbf{SVM} use the centre-pixel spectrum (LightGBM: 100 trees, \texttt{max\_delta\_step}$=1$, which prevented divergence on one Salinas split). Deep models receive a $15\times15$ patch. The \textbf{spectral transformer} uses only the centre pixel, one token per band (128-d, 4 layers, 4 heads). The \textbf{grouped-band transformer}, inspired by SpectralFormer \citep{Hong2022} without its cross-region fusion, also uses only the centre pixel, with four adjacent bands per token (64-d, 4 layers); bands that do not fill the last group are zero-padded (Pavia University and the water-band-removed soil variant). The \textbf{conv-stem transformer} is the spectral transformer with band tokens produced by two 3-D convolutions over the patch. \textbf{HybridSN} \citep{Roy2020} has three 3-D convolutions, one 2-D convolution and two dense layers; unlike the original, it uses all bands without PCA and a $15\times15$ window, so that every model sees the same input. All deep models use AdamW (learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), cosine annealing, batch size 64 and at most 50 epochs with early stopping (patience 10) on validation loss; on the disjoint splits every deep model is also rerun with up to 100 epochs. Inputs are standardised per band with training statistics. Runs used PyTorch 2.11, CUDA 12.8 and LightGBM 4.6 on Kaggle (NVIDIA Tesla T4 for the deep models).

\subsection{Protocols}\label{sec:protocol}
\emph{Random pixels (all scenes, leaky).} For each budget $p$ (as for the disjoint tiles below), $p$ of the labelled pixels are drawn by stratified sampling, 10\% of the rest (at most 3\,000 pixels on Pavia University and Salinas) form the validation set and the remainder the test set. Training and test windows overlap.

\emph{Disjoint tiles (all scenes, controlled).} Each scene is divided into square tiles (24 pixels for Indian Pines, 32 for the others), half of which form the training area. For each seed, the best of 400 random assignments is kept, ranked by the number of classes with $\ge$25\% of their pixels in the training area and $\ge$10\% in the test area. Test pixels within 7 pixels of a training tile are dropped, so no test window contains a training-area pixel. Training pixels are drawn from the training area in proportion to its class frequencies, to a total of $p$ of all labelled pixels ($\{1,5,10,20\}\%$ for Indian Pines, $\{0.5,1,2,5\}\%$ for the larger scenes); up to 3\,000 other training-area pixels form the validation set, used by the deep models for early stopping only. Because these are extra labels that the classical models do not use, HybridSN and the conv-stem transformer are also rerun with a matched budget: early stopping on 20\% of the $p$ training pixels (per class, rounded down), so that every model sees $p$ labels in total. Only classes present in the training area are scored: 10--13 of 16 on Indian Pines, all classes on the other scenes.

\emph{Soil patches.} Images of one sample are never split. In each of five repeats, the samples of each class are shuffled; 2 go to the test set, 1 to validation, and the budget $k\in\{1,2,3,4\}$ is the first $k$ remaining samples per class. At most 300 patches per training or validation sample are used. \emph{Raw}: every non-overlapping $15\times15$ patch (the protocol of most HSI pipelines). \emph{Soil-only}: a soil mask is computed per image (band-mean image divided by its column-wise 90th percentile, which removes vignetting; soil where the ratio is below 0.6), and only patches with $\ge$90\% soil are kept. \emph{Normalised}: soil-only patches with each spectrum divided by its mean over bands, which removes overall brightness.

\emph{Leakage checks.} \emph{Tray-only}: LightGBM and SVM are trained and tested on patches with $\le$10\% soil from the same samples (4 samples per class). \emph{Cross-state}: Red is the only class imaged both dry and moist; models are trained on Black, Yellow and one Red state and tested on the other Red state, with or without the water bands (932--1010 and 1344--1498\,nm) or brightness normalisation.

\emph{Statistics.} For each model we report the mean paired difference from LightGBM over the five seeds, its 95\% confidence interval and the paired $t$-test $p$-value adjusted with the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure ($q$). On the airborne scenes HybridSN is also compared with the random forest, the strongest classical baseline. We correct within two families: every test on the airborne scenes (both split protocols, 132 tests), and every model-versus-LightGBM test on the soil data (all three patch protocols, 36 tests). The 100-epoch reruns form a third family (60 tests) and the matched-budget reruns a fourth (48 tests, against LightGBM and the random forest).

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Spatial leakage}
Table~\ref{tab:ip} and Figure~\ref{fig:protocols} (left) compare the two splits of Indian Pines. With random pixels, HybridSN is the most accurate model at every budget and 16.6--20.4\pp\ ahead of LightGBM from 5\% ($q<0.001$); the conv-stem transformer is 9.6--13.7\pp\ ahead from 10\% ($q\le0.010$). With disjoint tiles, accuracy drops to 31--51\% and the ranking changes. LightGBM has the highest mean at 1--10\%, and no deep model is significantly better than it at any budget. The two centre-pixel transformers are significantly worse at 5\% ($-9.3$ and $-11.3$\pp, $q\le0.020$), the spectral transformer also at 10\%, and at 1\% the random forest (41.3\%) is significantly more accurate than HybridSN ($+6.4$\pp, $q=0.016$). At 20\%, HybridSN falls from 99.2\% to 45.2\%. Part of this drop reflects a harder test set: classes that cannot be split are not scored, and test pixels are spatially separated. The loss of HybridSN's advantage, however, is an effect of the protocol.

On Pavia University and Salinas the random split also inflates accuracy (Supplementary Table~S2): by 3.8--9.0\pp\ for LightGBM and 3.2--10.5\pp\ for HybridSN. It also shrinks the seed-to-seed SD of HybridSN from 1.2--5.6\pp\ to 0.1--2.2\pp. Here, however, the ranking holds: HybridSN is the most accurate model under both protocols, and its lead over LightGBM is similar (random: $+1.0$ to $+9.7$\pp; disjoint: $+3.1$ to $+9.1$\pp). Spatial leakage therefore changed the conclusion only on Indian Pines, plausibly because its large homogeneous fields put most randomly drawn test windows next to training pixels of the same field.

\subsection{Disjoint benchmark on three scenes}
Under disjoint tiles the advantage of patch-based models depends on the scene (Table~\ref{tab:scenes}, Figure~\ref{fig:scenes}). On Pavia University and Salinas, HybridSN is the most accurate model at every budget, 6.9--9.1\pp\ above LightGBM on Pavia University and 3.1--8.2\pp\ on Salinas (significant in six of eight cases, $q\le0.049$). It is also significantly ahead of the random forest at every budget on Pavia University ($+5.7$ to $+8.7$\pp) and from 1\% on Salinas ($+5.7$ to $+7.0$\pp), but level with it at 0.5\% on Salinas ($+0.3$\pp). The random forest matches or exceeds LightGBM at the smallest budgets (significantly on Salinas at 0.5\% and 1\%), and the SVM is the weakest classical model. The conv-stem transformer is significantly ahead of LightGBM at 2\% on both scenes and at 5\% on Salinas. The centre-pixel transformers are never significantly better than LightGBM; the grouped-band transformer is significantly worse on Pavia University at 0.5\%, 2\% and 5\%. Average accuracy and Cohen's $\kappa$ give the same best model in every row except one (Supplementary Table~S1).

\emph{Training length.} Many 50-epoch runs stopped at the epoch limit; with up to 100 epochs at most 40\% did, and mean accuracy changed by $-4.1$ to $+8.3$\pp. The conclusions did not change: on Indian Pines no deep model was significantly better than LightGBM (HybridSN was now significantly worse at 5\%, $-7.3$\pp, $q=0.039$); on Pavia University and Salinas HybridSN led LightGBM at every budget ($+6.2$ to $+9.8$\pp, significant in six of eight cases) and the random forest ($+5.3$ to $+9.4$\pp).

\emph{Matched label budget.} Without the extra validation pixels, HybridSN remained ahead of LightGBM on Pavia University and Salinas at seven of eight budgets ($+5.1$ to $+8.3$\pp; significant at four, $q\le0.030$) and of the random forest at the same seven ($+3.9$ to $+7.4$\pp; significant at six, $q\le0.044$). On Indian Pines, HybridSN was again not significantly different from LightGBM, and the conv-stem transformer was significantly worse at 5\% and 10\% ($-12.0$ and $-10.6$\pp, $q=0.044$).

\subsection{Acquisition leakage in laboratory soil images}
In the soil images 38--72\% of the non-overlapping patches contain $\le$10\% soil (measured on 24 of the 36 images: 4 Black, 4 Yellow and all 16 Red). Trained and tested only on such tray patches, LightGBM reaches $87.1\pm2.0\%$ and the SVM $78.2\pm2.3\%$ (chance 33\%): the tray identifies the imaging session, and with it the class.

The patch protocol changes both accuracy and ranking (Table~\ref{tab:soil}, Figure~\ref{fig:protocols}, right). With raw patches, accuracy rises slowly with the budget. HybridSN is significantly ahead of LightGBM with four samples per class ($+8.8$\pp, $q=0.022$), and the SVM is significantly worse than LightGBM at every budget ($-6.6$ to $-11.5$\pp). With soil-only patches every model exceeds 97\% from three samples per class; HybridSN's lead shrinks to $+2.2$\pp, and the SVM is now slightly ahead of LightGBM ($+1.1$\pp, $q=0.039$). After brightness normalisation every model reaches at least 93.6\% with one sample per class and 98\% with two, and the only remaining significant difference is HybridSN at two samples per class ($+1.0$\pp, $q=0.005$).

\subsection{Band importance}
Exact TreeSHAP values \citep{Lundberg2020} for LightGBM are plotted at the band centres from the sensor metadata in Figure~\ref{fig:shap}. On soil-only patches one band dominates: 1414\,nm, on the first overtone of the O--H stretch of water and hydroxyl, with 3.7 times the importance of the next band (1176\,nm). Because Black soil was imaged only moist and Yellow only dry, this weight may reflect moisture rather than mineralogy. On Indian Pines (5\% labelled, disjoint split) the top bands are at 400\,nm (the first band, where noise is high), 735\,nm (the red edge of vegetation) and 2083\,nm.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\emph{The protocol can overturn the conclusion.} On Indian Pines the random split supports the familiar claim that 3-D CNNs outperform gradient boosting by about 20\pp; the disjoint split does not. On Pavia University and Salinas the random split inflates LightGBM and HybridSN by similar amounts and keeps the ranking. The size of the leak therefore depends on the scene and cannot be assumed small in advance. On the soil images, raw patches suggest that HybridSN pays off at larger budgets and that the SVM is a poor choice; once the tray is removed and brightness normalised, all learners are within two points of each other from two samples per class. Neither leak is specific to one model: spatial leakage favours patch-based models, while acquisition leakage rewards any model that can read session cues.

\emph{What survives controlled evaluation.} On Pavia University and Salinas under disjoint tiles, HybridSN leads LightGBM and the random forest, also when it gets no labels beyond the training budget (except at the smallest Salinas budget). This is consistent with the value of a local inductive bias \citep{Hassani2021}. The conv-stem transformer's advantage largely depended on the extra validation labels, so the size of the validation set should be counted in the label budget. Transformers that see only the centre spectrum never beat LightGBM significantly, consistent with the strength of tree ensembles on per-pixel input \citep{Grinsztajn2022}.

\emph{Checklist.} Before ranking models with few labels: (1) split spatially, with a buffer at least half the patch size; (2) for laboratory images, mask the background and report the share of background patches; (3) train a classifier on background-only patches, and treat above-chance accuracy as leakage; (4) report whether class is confounded with acquisition session, and test across sessions where any class allows it; (5) compare models under brightness normalisation or radiometric calibration; (6) include more than one classical baseline, since a random forest outperformed LightGBM at the smallest budgets; (7) count validation labels used for early stopping or tuning in the label budget.

\emph{Limitations.} The soil dataset has 22 samples, raw digital numbers and class confounded with session; only Red allows a cross-session test. Five seeds give wide intervals, and because repeated splits share data, the paired $t$-tests are approximate \citep{Nadeau2003}; a non-significant difference is not evidence of equal accuracy. All models use one training protocol without per-model tuning; the 100-epoch check covers the disjoint splits but not the random split or the soil data. On Indian Pines the disjoint split scores only the classes present in the training area.

\section{Conclusion}
With few labels, the ranking of hyperspectral classifiers depended on the evaluation design as much as on the models. A random pixel split made HybridSN 17--20\pp\ better than LightGBM on Indian Pines from 5\% labelled pixels; disjoint tiles removed the advantage. On Pavia University and Salinas the random split inflated accuracy by up to 11\pp\ without changing the ranking, and HybridSN remained better under disjoint tiles. In laboratory soil images, background and brightness cues let a tray-only classifier reach 87\%; after masking and normalisation all models averaged about 94\% or more with one labelled sample per class. Spatially disjoint splits, background masking and leakage checks should precede any claim that one model family needs fewer labels than another.
